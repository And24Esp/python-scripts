   8 CHAPTER I. Down the Rabbit-Hole
   9 
  10 Alice was beginning to get very tired of sitting by her sister on the
  11 bank, and of having nothing to do: once or twice she had peeped into the
  12 book her sister was reading, but it had no pictures or conversations in
  13 it, 'and what is the use of a book,' thought Alice 'without pictures or
  14 conversation?'
  15 
  16 So she was considering in her own mind (as well as she could, for the
  17 hot day made her feel very sleepy and stupid), whether the pleasure
  18 of making a daisy-chain would be worth the trouble of getting up and
  19 picking the daisies, when suddenly a White Rabbit with pink eyes ran
  20 close by her.
  21 
  22 There was nothing so VERY remarkable in that; nor did Alice think it so
  23 VERY much out of the way to hear the Rabbit say to itself, 'Oh dear!
  24 Oh dear! I shall be late!' (when she thought it over afterwards, it
  25 occurred to her that she ought to have wondered at this, but at the time
  26 it all seemed quite natural); but when the Rabbit actually TOOK A WATCH
  27 OUT OF ITS WAISTCOAT-POCKET, and looked at it, and then hurried on,
  28 Alice started to her feet, for it flashed across her mind that she had
  29 never before seen a rabbit with either a waistcoat-pocket, or a watch
  30 to take out of it, and burning with curiosity, she ran across the field
  31 after it, and fortunately was just in time to see it pop down a large
  32 rabbit-hole under the hedge.
  33 
  34 In another moment down went Alice after it, never once considering how
  35 in the world she was to get out again.
  36 
  37 The rabbit-hole went straight on like a tunnel for some way, and then
  38 dipped suddenly down, so suddenly that Alice had not a moment to think
  39 about stopping herself before she found herself falling down a very deep
  40 well.
  41 
  42 Either the well was very deep, or she fell very slowly, for she had
  43 plenty of time as she went down to look about her and to wonder what was
  44 going to happen next. First, she tried to look down and make out what
  45 she was coming to, but it was too dark to see anything; then she
  46 looked at the sides of the well, and noticed that they were filled with
  47 cupboards and book-shelves; here and there she saw maps and pictures
  48 hung upon pegs. She took down a jar from one of the shelves as
  49 she passed; it was labelled 'ORANGE MARMALADE', but to her great
  50 disappointment it was empty: she did not like to drop the jar for fear
  51 of killing somebody, so managed to put it into one of the cupboards as
  52 she fell past it.
  53 
  54 'Well!' thought Alice to herself, 'after such a fall as this, I shall
  55 think nothing of tumbling down stairs! How brave they'll all think me at
  56 home! Why, I wouldn't say anything about it, even if I fell off the top
  57 of the house!' (Which was very likely true.)
  58 
  59 Down, down, down. Would the fall NEVER come to an end! 'I wonder how
  60 many miles I've fallen by this time?' she said aloud. 'I must be getting
  61 somewhere near the centre of the earth. Let me see: that would be four
  62 thousand miles down, I think--' (for, you see, Alice had learnt several
  63 things of this sort in her lessons in the schoolroom, and though this
  64 was not a VERY good opportunity for showing off her knowledge, as there
  65 was no one to listen to her, still it was good practice to say it over)
  66 '--yes, that's about the right distance--but then I wonder what Latitude
  67 or Longitude I've got to?' (Alice had no idea what Latitude was, or
  68 Longitude either, but thought they were nice grand words to say.)
  69 
  70 Presently she began again. 'I wonder if I shall fall right THROUGH the
  71 earth! How funny it'll seem to come out among the people that walk with
  72 their heads downward! The Antipathies, I think--' (she was rather glad
  73 there WAS no one listening, this time, as it didn't sound at all the
  74 right word) '--but I shall have to ask them what the name of the country
  75 is, you know. Please, Ma'am, is this New Zealand or Australia?' (and
  76 she tried to curtsey as she spoke--fancy CURTSEYING as you're falling
  77 through the air! Do you think you could manage it?) 'And what an
  78 ignorant little girl she'll think me for asking! No, it'll never do to
  79 ask: perhaps I shall see it written up somewhere.'
  80 
  81 Down, down, down. There was nothing else to do, so Alice soon began
  82 talking again. 'Dinah'll miss me very much to-night, I should think!'
  83 (Dinah was the cat.) 'I hope they'll remember her saucer of milk at
  84 tea-time. Dinah my dear! I wish you were down here with me! There are no
  85 mice in the air, I'm afraid, but you might catch a bat, and that's very
  86 like a mouse, you know. But do cats eat bats, I wonder?' And here Alice
  87 began to get rather sleepy, and went on saying to herself, in a dreamy
  88 sort of way, 'Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?' and sometimes, 'Do
  89 bats eat cats?' for, you see, as she couldn't answer either question,
  90 it didn't much matter which way she put it. She felt that she was dozing
  91 off, and had just begun to dream that she was walking hand in hand with
  92 Dinah, and saying to her very earnestly, 'Now, Dinah, tell me the truth:
  93 did you ever eat a bat?' when suddenly, thump! thump! down she came upon
  94 a heap of sticks and dry leaves, and the fall was over.
  95 
  96 Alice was not a bit hurt, and she jumped up on to her feet in a moment:
  97 she looked up, but it was all dark overhead; before her was another
  98 long passage, and the White Rabbit was still in sight, hurrying down it.
  99 There was not a moment to be lost: away went Alice like the wind, and
 100 was just in time to hear it say, as it turned a corner, 'Oh my ears
 101 and whiskers, how late it's getting!' She was close behind it when she
 102 turned the corner, but the Rabbit was no longer to be seen: she found
 103 herself in a long, low hall, which was lit up by a row of lamps hanging
 104 from the roof.
 105 
 106 There were doors all round the hall, but they were all locked; and when
 107 Alice had been all the way down one side and up the other, trying every
 108 door, she walked sadly down the middle, wondering how she was ever to
 109 get out again.
 110 
 111 Suddenly she came upon a little three-legged table, all made of solid
 112 glass; there was nothing on it except a tiny golden key, and Alice's
 113 first thought was that it might belong to one of the doors of the hall;
 114 but, alas! either the locks were too large, or the key was too small,
 115 but at any rate it would not open any of them. However, on the second
 116 time round, she came upon a low curtain she had not noticed before, and
 117 behind it was a little door about fifteen inches high: she tried the
 118 little golden key in the lock, and to her great delight it fitted!
 119 
 120 Alice opened the door and found that it led into a small passage, not
 121 much larger than a rat-hole: she knelt down and looked along the passage
 122 into the loveliest garden you ever saw. How she longed to get out of
 123 that dark hall, and wander about among those beds of bright flowers and
 124 those cool fountains, but she could not even get her head through the
 125 doorway; 'and even if my head would go through,' thought poor Alice, 'it
 126 would be of very little use without my shoulders. Oh, how I wish I could
 127 shut up like a telescope! I think I could, if I only know how to begin.'
 128 For, you see, so many out-of-the-way things had happened lately,
 129 that Alice had begun to think that very few things indeed were really
 130 impossible.
 131 
 132 There seemed to be no use in waiting by the little door, so she went
 133 back to the table, half hoping she might find another key on it, or at
 134 any rate a book of rules for shutting people up like telescopes: this
 135 time she found a little bottle on it, ('which certainly was not here
 136 before,' said Alice,) and round the neck of the bottle was a paper
 137 label, with the words 'DRINK ME' beautifully printed on it in large
 138 letters.
 139 
 140 It was all very well to say 'Drink me,' but the wise little Alice was
 141 not going to do THAT in a hurry. 'No, I'll look first,' she said, 'and
 142 see whether it's marked "poison" or not'; for she had read several nice
 143 little histories about children who had got burnt, and eaten up by wild
 144 beasts and other unpleasant things, all because they WOULD not remember
 145 the simple rules their friends had taught them: such as, that a red-hot
 146 poker will burn you if you hold it too long; and that if you cut your
 147 finger VERY deeply with a knife, it usually bleeds; and she had never
 148 forgotten that, if you drink much from a bottle marked 'poison,' it is
 149 almost certain to disagree with you, sooner or later.
 150 
 151 However, this bottle was NOT marked 'poison,' so Alice ventured to taste
 152 it, and finding it very nice, (it had, in fact, a sort of mixed flavour
 153 of cherry-tart, custard, pine-apple, roast turkey, toffee, and hot
 154 buttered toast,) she very soon finished it off.
 155 
 156   *    *    *    *    *    *    *
 157 
 158     *    *    *    *    *    *
 159 
 160   *    *    *    *    *    *    *
 161 
 162 'What a curious feeling!' said Alice; 'I must be shutting up like a
 163 telescope.'
 164 
 165 And so it was indeed: she was now only ten inches high, and her face
 166 brightened up at the thought that she was now the right size for going
 167 through the little door into that lovely garden. First, however, she
 168 waited for a few minutes to see if she was going to shrink any further:
 169 she felt a little nervous about this; 'for it might end, you know,' said
 170 Alice to herself, 'in my going out altogether, like a candle. I wonder
 171 what I should be like then?' And she tried to fancy what the flame of a
 172 candle is like after the candle is blown out, for she could not remember
 173 ever having seen such a thing.
 174 
 175 After a while, finding that nothing more happened, she decided on going
 176 into the garden at once; but, alas for poor Alice! when she got to the
 177 door, she found she had forgotten the little golden key, and when she
 178 went back to the table for it, she found she could not possibly reach
 179 it: she could see it quite plainly through the glass, and she tried her
 180 best to climb up one of the legs of the table, but it was too slippery;
 181 and when she had tired herself out with trying, the poor little thing
 182 sat down and cried.
 183 
 184 'Come, there's no use in crying like that!' said Alice to herself,
 185 rather sharply; 'I advise you to leave off this minute!' She generally
 186 gave herself very good advice, (though she very seldom followed it),
 187 and sometimes she scolded herself so severely as to bring tears into
 188 her eyes; and once she remembered trying to box her own ears for having
 189 cheated herself in a game of croquet she was playing against herself,
 190 for this curious child was very fond of pretending to be two people.
 191 'But it's no use now,' thought poor Alice, 'to pretend to be two people!
 192 Why, there's hardly enough of me left to make ONE respectable person!'
 193 
 194 Soon her eye fell on a little glass box that was lying under the table:
 195 she opened it, and found in it a very small cake, on which the words
 196 'EAT ME' were beautifully marked in currants. 'Well, I'll eat it,' said
 197 Alice, 'and if it makes me grow larger, I can reach the key; and if it
 198 makes me grow smaller, I can creep under the door; so either way I'll
 199 get into the garden, and I don't care which happens!'
 200 
 201 She ate a little bit, and said anxiously to herself, 'Which way? Which
 202 way?', holding her hand on the top of her head to feel which way it was
 203 growing, and she was quite surprised to find that she remained the same
 204 size: to be sure, this generally happens when one eats cake, but Alice
 205 had got so much into the way of expecting nothing but out-of-the-way
 206 things to happen, that it seemed quite dull and stupid for life to go on
 207 in the common way.
 208 
 209 So she set to work, and very soon finished off the cake.

 220 CHAPTER II. The Pool of Tears
 221 
 222 'Curiouser and curiouser!' cried Alice (she was so much surprised, that
 223 for the moment she quite forgot how to speak good English); 'now I'm
 224 opening out like the largest telescope that ever was! Good-bye, feet!'
 225 (for when she looked down at her feet, they seemed to be almost out of
 226 sight, they were getting so far off). 'Oh, my poor little feet, I wonder
 227 who will put on your shoes and stockings for you now, dears? I'm sure
 228 _I_ shan't be able! I shall be a great deal too far off to trouble
 229 myself about you: you must manage the best way you can;--but I must be
 230 kind to them,' thought Alice, 'or perhaps they won't walk the way I want
 231 to go! Let me see: I'll give them a new pair of boots every Christmas.'
 232 
 233 And she went on planning to herself how she would manage it. 'They must
 234 go by the carrier,' she thought; 'and how funny it'll seem, sending
 235 presents to one's own feet! And how odd the directions will look!
 236 
 237      ALICE'S RIGHT FOOT, ESQ.
 238        HEARTHRUG,
 239          NEAR THE FENDER,
 240            (WITH ALICE'S LOVE).
 241 
 242 Oh dear, what nonsense I'm talking!'
 243 
 244 Just then her head struck against the roof of the hall: in fact she was
 245 now more than nine feet high, and she at once took up the little golden
 246 key and hurried off to the garden door.
 247 
 248 Poor Alice! It was as much as she could do, lying down on one side, to
 249 look through into the garden with one eye; but to get through was more
 250 hopeless than ever: she sat down and began to cry again.
 251 
 252 'You ought to be ashamed of yourself,' said Alice, 'a great girl like
 253 you,' (she might well say this), 'to go on crying in this way! Stop this
 254 moment, I tell you!' But she went on all the same, shedding gallons of
 255 tears, until there was a large pool all round her, about four inches
 256 deep and reaching half down the hall.
 257 
 258 After a time she heard a little pattering of feet in the distance, and
 259 she hastily dried her eyes to see what was coming. It was the White
 260 Rabbit returning, splendidly dressed, with a pair of white kid gloves in
 261 one hand and a large fan in the other: he came trotting along in a great
 262 hurry, muttering to himself as he came, 'Oh! the Duchess, the Duchess!
 263 Oh! won't she be savage if I've kept her waiting!' Alice felt so
 264 desperate that she was ready to ask help of any one; so, when the Rabbit
 265 came near her, she began, in a low, timid voice, 'If you please, sir--'
 266 The Rabbit started violently, dropped the white kid gloves and the fan,
 267 and skurried away into the darkness as hard as he could go.
 268 
 269 Alice took up the fan and gloves, and, as the hall was very hot, she
 270 kept fanning herself all the time she went on talking: 'Dear, dear! How
 271 queer everything is to-day! And yesterday things went on just as usual.
 272 I wonder if I've been changed in the night? Let me think: was I the
 273 same when I got up this morning? I almost think I can remember feeling a
 274 little different. But if I'm not the same, the next question is, Who
 275 in the world am I? Ah, THAT'S the great puzzle!' And she began thinking
 276 over all the children she knew that were of the same age as herself, to
 277 see if she could have been changed for any of them.
 278 
 279 'I'm sure I'm not Ada,' she said, 'for her hair goes in such long
 280 ringlets, and mine doesn't go in ringlets at all; and I'm sure I can't
 281 be Mabel, for I know all sorts of things, and she, oh! she knows such a
 282 very little! Besides, SHE'S she, and I'm I, and--oh dear, how puzzling
 283 it all is! I'll try if I know all the things I used to know. Let me
 284 see: four times five is twelve, and four times six is thirteen, and
 285 four times seven is--oh dear! I shall never get to twenty at that rate!
 286 However, the Multiplication Table doesn't signify: let's try Geography.
 287 London is the capital of Paris, and Paris is the capital of Rome, and
 288 Rome--no, THAT'S all wrong, I'm certain! I must have been changed for
 289 Mabel! I'll try and say "How doth the little--"' and she crossed her
 290 hands on her lap as if she were saying lessons, and began to repeat it,
 291 but her voice sounded hoarse and strange, and the words did not come the
 292 same as they used to do:--
 293 
 294      'How doth the little crocodile
 295       Improve his shining tail,
 296      And pour the waters of the Nile
 297       On every golden scale!
 298 
 299      'How cheerfully he seems to grin,
 300       How neatly spread his claws,
 301      And welcome little fishes in
 302       With gently smiling jaws!'
 303 
 304 'I'm sure those are not the right words,' said poor Alice, and her eyes
 305 filled with tears again as she went on, 'I must be Mabel after all, and
 306 I shall have to go and live in that poky little house, and have next to
 307 no toys to play with, and oh! ever so many lessons to learn! No, I've
 308 made up my mind about it; if I'm Mabel, I'll stay down here! It'll be no
 309 use their putting their heads down and saying "Come up again, dear!" I
 310 shall only look up and say "Who am I then? Tell me that first, and then,
 311 if I like being that person, I'll come up: if not, I'll stay down here
 312 till I'm somebody else"--but, oh dear!' cried Alice, with a sudden burst
 313 of tears, 'I do wish they WOULD put their heads down! I am so VERY tired
 314 of being all alone here!'
 315 
 316 As she said this she looked down at her hands, and was surprised to see
 317 that she had put on one of the Rabbit's little white kid gloves while
 318 she was talking. 'How CAN I have done that?' she thought. 'I must
 319 be growing small again.' She got up and went to the table to measure
 320 herself by it, and found that, as nearly as she could guess, she was now
 321 about two feet high, and was going on shrinking rapidly: she soon found
 322 out that the cause of this was the fan she was holding, and she dropped
 323 it hastily, just in time to avoid shrinking away altogether.
 324 
 325 'That WAS a narrow escape!' said Alice, a good deal frightened at the
 326 sudden change, but very glad to find herself still in existence; 'and
 327 now for the garden!' and she ran with all speed back to the little door:
 328 but, alas! the little door was shut again, and the little golden key was
 329 lying on the glass table as before, 'and things are worse than ever,'
 330 thought the poor child, 'for I never was so small as this before, never!
 331 And I declare it's too bad, that it is!'
 332 
 333 As she said these words her foot slipped, and in another moment, splash!
 334 she was up to her chin in salt water. Her first idea was that she
 335 had somehow fallen into the sea, 'and in that case I can go back by
 336 railway,' she said to herself. (Alice had been to the seaside once in
 337 her life, and had come to the general conclusion, that wherever you go
 338 to on the English coast you find a number of bathing machines in the
 339 sea, some children digging in the sand with wooden spades, then a row
 340 of lodging houses, and behind them a railway station.) However, she soon
 341 made out that she was in the pool of tears which she had wept when she
 342 was nine feet high.
 343 
 344 'I wish I hadn't cried so much!' said Alice, as she swam about, trying
 345 to find her way out. 'I shall be punished for it now, I suppose, by
 346 being drowned in my own tears! That WILL be a queer thing, to be sure!
 347 However, everything is queer to-day.'
 348 
 349 Just then she heard something splashing about in the pool a little way
 350 off, and she swam nearer to make out what it was: at first she thought
 351 it must be a walrus or hippopotamus, but then she remembered how small
 352 she was now, and she soon made out that it was only a mouse that had
 353 slipped in like herself.
 354 
 355 'Would it be of any use, now,' thought Alice, 'to speak to this mouse?
 356 Everything is so out-of-the-way down here, that I should think very
 357 likely it can talk: at any rate, there's no harm in trying.' So she
 358 began: 'O Mouse, do you know the way out of this pool? I am very tired
 359 of swimming about here, O Mouse!' (Alice thought this must be the right
 360 way of speaking to a mouse: she had never done such a thing before, but
 361 she remembered having seen in her brother's Latin Grammar, 'A mouse--of
 362 a mouse--to a mouse--a mouse--O mouse!') The Mouse looked at her rather
 363 inquisitively, and seemed to her to wink with one of its little eyes,
 364 but it said nothing.
 365 
 366 'Perhaps it doesn't understand English,' thought Alice; 'I daresay it's
 367 a French mouse, come over with William the Conqueror.' (For, with all
 368 her knowledge of history, Alice had no very clear notion how long ago
 369 anything had happened.) So she began again: 'Ou est ma chatte?' which
 370 was the first sentence in her French lesson-book. The Mouse gave a
 371 sudden leap out of the water, and seemed to quiver all over with fright.
 372 'Oh, I beg your pardon!' cried Alice hastily, afraid that she had hurt
 373 the poor animal's feelings. 'I quite forgot you didn't like cats.'
 374 
 375 'Not like cats!' cried the Mouse, in a shrill, passionate voice. 'Would
 376 YOU like cats if you were me?'
 377 
 378 'Well, perhaps not,' said Alice in a soothing tone: 'don't be angry
 379 about it. And yet I wish I could show you our cat Dinah: I think you'd
 380 take a fancy to cats if you could only see her. She is such a dear quiet
 381 thing,' Alice went on, half to herself, as she swam lazily about in the
 382 pool, 'and she sits purring so nicely by the fire, licking her paws and
 383 washing her face--and she is such a nice soft thing to nurse--and she's
 384 such a capital one for catching mice--oh, I beg your pardon!' cried
 385 Alice again, for this time the Mouse was bristling all over, and she
 386 felt certain it must be really offended. 'We won't talk about her any
 387 more if you'd rather not.'
 388 
 389 'We indeed!' cried the Mouse, who was trembling down to the end of his
 390 tail. 'As if I would talk on such a subject! Our family always HATED
 391 cats: nasty, low, vulgar things! Don't let me hear the name again!'
 392 
 393 'I won't indeed!' said Alice, in a great hurry to change the subject of
 394 conversation. 'Are you--are you fond--of--of dogs?' The Mouse did not
 395 answer, so Alice went on eagerly: 'There is such a nice little dog near
 396 our house I should like to show you! A little bright-eyed terrier, you
 397 know, with oh, such long curly brown hair! And it'll fetch things when
 398 you throw them, and it'll sit up and beg for its dinner, and all sorts
 399 of things--I can't remember half of them--and it belongs to a farmer,
 400 you know, and he says it's so useful, it's worth a hundred pounds! He
 401 says it kills all the rats and--oh dear!' cried Alice in a sorrowful
 402 tone, 'I'm afraid I've offended it again!' For the Mouse was swimming
 403 away from her as hard as it could go, and making quite a commotion in
 404 the pool as it went.
 405 
 406 So she called softly after it, 'Mouse dear! Do come back again, and we
 407 won't talk about cats or dogs either, if you don't like them!' When the
 408 Mouse heard this, it turned round and swam slowly back to her: its
 409 face was quite pale (with passion, Alice thought), and it said in a low
 410 trembling voice, 'Let us get to the shore, and then I'll tell you my
 411 history, and you'll understand why it is I hate cats and dogs.'
 412 
 413 It was high time to go, for the pool was getting quite crowded with the
 414 birds and animals that had fallen into it: there were a Duck and a Dodo,
 415 a Lory and an Eaglet, and several other curious creatures. Alice led the
 416 way, and the whole party swam to the shore.
 417

 421 CHAPTER III. A Caucus-Race and a Long Tale
 422 
 423 They were indeed a queer-looking party that assembled on the bank--the
 424 birds with draggled feathers, the animals with their fur clinging close
 425 to them, and all dripping wet, cross, and uncomfortable.
 426 
 427 The first question of course was, how to get dry again: they had a
 428 consultation about this, and after a few minutes it seemed quite natural
 429 to Alice to find herself talking familiarly with them, as if she had
 430 known them all her life. Indeed, she had quite a long argument with the
 431 Lory, who at last turned sulky, and would only say, 'I am older than
 432 you, and must know better'; and this Alice would not allow without
 433 knowing how old it was, and, as the Lory positively refused to tell its
 434 age, there was no more to be said.
 435 
 436 At last the Mouse, who seemed to be a person of authority among them,
 437 called out, 'Sit down, all of you, and listen to me! I'LL soon make you
 438 dry enough!' They all sat down at once, in a large ring, with the Mouse
 439 in the middle. Alice kept her eyes anxiously fixed on it, for she felt
 440 sure she would catch a bad cold if she did not get dry very soon.
 441 
 442 'Ahem!' said the Mouse with an important air, 'are you all ready? This
 443 is the driest thing I know. Silence all round, if you please! "William
 444 the Conqueror, whose cause was favoured by the pope, was soon submitted
 445 to by the English, who wanted leaders, and had been of late much
 446 accustomed to usurpation and conquest. Edwin and Morcar, the earls of
 447 Mercia and Northumbria--"'
 448 
 449 'Ugh!' said the Lory, with a shiver.
 450 
 451 'I beg your pardon!' said the Mouse, frowning, but very politely: 'Did
 452 you speak?'
 453 
 454 'Not I!' said the Lory hastily.
 455 
 456 'I thought you did,' said the Mouse. '--I proceed. "Edwin and Morcar,
 457 the earls of Mercia and Northumbria, declared for him: and even Stigand,
 458 the patriotic archbishop of Canterbury, found it advisable--"'
 459 
 460 'Found WHAT?' said the Duck.
 461 
 462 'Found IT,' the Mouse replied rather crossly: 'of course you know what
 463 "it" means.'
 464 
 465 'I know what "it" means well enough, when I find a thing,' said the
 466 Duck: 'it's generally a frog or a worm. The question is, what did the
 467 archbishop find?'
 468 
 469 The Mouse did not notice this question, but hurriedly went on, '"--found
 470 it advisable to go with Edgar Atheling to meet William and offer him the
 471 crown. William's conduct at first was moderate. But the insolence of his
 472 Normans--" How are you getting on now, my dear?' it continued, turning
 473 to Alice as it spoke.
 474 
 475 'As wet as ever,' said Alice in a melancholy tone: 'it doesn't seem to
 476 dry me at all.'
 477 
 478 'In that case,' said the Dodo solemnly, rising to its feet, 'I move
 479 that the meeting adjourn, for the immediate adoption of more energetic
 480 remedies--'
 481 
 482 'Speak English!' said the Eaglet. 'I don't know the meaning of half
 483 those long words, and, what's more, I don't believe you do either!' And
 484 the Eaglet bent down its head to hide a smile: some of the other birds
 485 tittered audibly.
 486 
 487 'What I was going to say,' said the Dodo in an offended tone, 'was, that
 488 the best thing to get us dry would be a Caucus-race.'
 489 
 490 'What IS a Caucus-race?' said Alice; not that she wanted much to know,
 491 but the Dodo had paused as if it thought that SOMEBODY ought to speak,
 492 and no one else seemed inclined to say anything.
 493 
 494 'Why,' said the Dodo, 'the best way to explain it is to do it.' (And, as
 495 you might like to try the thing yourself, some winter day, I will tell
 496 you how the Dodo managed it.)
 497 
 498 First it marked out a race-course, in a sort of circle, ('the exact
 499 shape doesn't matter,' it said,) and then all the party were placed
 500 along the course, here and there. There was no 'One, two, three, and
 501 away,' but they began running when they liked, and left off when they
 502 liked, so that it was not easy to know when the race was over. However,
 503 when they had been running half an hour or so, and were quite dry again,
 504 the Dodo suddenly called out 'The race is over!' and they all crowded
 505 round it, panting, and asking, 'But who has won?'
 506 
 507 This question the Dodo could not answer without a great deal of thought,
 508 and it sat for a long time with one finger pressed upon its forehead
 509 (the position in which you usually see Shakespeare, in the pictures
 510 of him), while the rest waited in silence. At last the Dodo said,
 511 'EVERYBODY has won, and all must have prizes.'
 512

 665 CHAPTER IV. The Rabbit Sends in a Little Bill
 666 
 667 It was the White Rabbit, trotting slowly back again, and looking
 668 anxiously about as it went, as if it had lost something; and she heard
 669 it muttering to itself 'The Duchess! The Duchess! Oh my dear paws! Oh
 670 my fur and whiskers! She'll get me executed, as sure as ferrets are
 671 ferrets! Where CAN I have dropped them, I wonder?' Alice guessed in a
 672 moment that it was looking for the fan and the pair of white kid gloves,
 673 and she very good-naturedly began hunting about for them, but they were
 674 nowhere to be seen--everything seemed to have changed since her swim in
 675 the pool, and the great hall, with the glass table and the little door,
 676 had vanished completely.
 677 
 678 Very soon the Rabbit noticed Alice, as she went hunting about, and
 679 called out to her in an angry tone, 'Why, Mary Ann, what ARE you doing
 680 out here? Run home this moment, and fetch me a pair of gloves and a fan!
 681 Quick, now!' And Alice was so much frightened that she ran off at once
 682 in the direction it pointed to, without trying to explain the mistake it
 683 had made.
 684 
 685 'He took me for his housemaid,' she said to herself as she ran. 'How
 686 surprised he'll be when he finds out who I am! But I'd better take him
 687 his fan and gloves--that is, if I can find them.' As she said this, she
 688 came upon a neat little house, on the door of which was a bright brass
 689 plate with the name 'W. RABBIT' engraved upon it. She went in without
 690 knocking, and hurried upstairs, in great fear lest she should meet the
 691 real Mary Ann, and be turned out of the house before she had found the
 692 fan and gloves.
 693 
 694 'How queer it seems,' Alice said to herself, 'to be going messages for
 695 a rabbit! I suppose Dinah'll be sending me on messages next!' And she
 696 began fancying the sort of thing that would happen: '"Miss Alice! Come
 697 here directly, and get ready for your walk!" "Coming in a minute,
 698 nurse! But I've got to see that the mouse doesn't get out." Only I don't
 699 think,' Alice went on, 'that they'd let Dinah stop in the house if it
 700 began ordering people about like that!'
 701 
 702 By this time she had found her way into a tidy little room with a table
 703 in the window, and on it (as she had hoped) a fan and two or three pairs
 704 of tiny white kid gloves: she took up the fan and a pair of the gloves,
 705 and was just going to leave the room, when her eye fell upon a little
 706 bottle that stood near the looking-glass. There was no label this time
 707 with the words 'DRINK ME,' but nevertheless she uncorked it and put it
 708 to her lips. 'I know SOMETHING interesting is sure to happen,' she said
 709 to herself, 'whenever I eat or drink anything; so I'll just see what
 710 this bottle does. I do hope it'll make me grow large again, for really
 711 I'm quite tired of being such a tiny little thing!'
 712 
 713 It did so indeed, and much sooner than she had expected: before she had
 714 drunk half the bottle, she found her head pressing against the ceiling,

 716 down the bottle, saying to herself 'That's quite enough--I hope I shan't
 717 grow any more--As it is, I can't get out at the door--I do wish I hadn't
 718 drunk quite so much!'
 719 
 720 Alas! it was too late to wish that! She went on growing, and growing,
 721 and very soon had to kneel down on the floor: in another minute there
 722 was not even room for this, and she tried the effect of lying down with
 723 one elbow against the door, and the other arm curled round her head.

 725 of the window, and one foot up the chimney, and said to herself 'Now I
 726 can do no more, whatever happens. What WILL become of me?'
 727 
 728 Luckily for Alice, the little magic bottle had now had its full effect,
 729 and she grew no larger: still it was very uncomfortable, and, as there
 730 seemed to be no sort of chance of her ever getting out of the room
 731 again, no wonder she felt unhappy.
 732 
 733 'It was much pleasanter at home,' thought poor Alice, 'when one wasn't
 734 always growing larger and smaller, and being ordered about by mice and
 735 rabbits. I almost wish I hadn't gone down that rabbit-hole--and yet--and

 737 CAN have happened to me! When I used to read fairy-tales, I fancied that
 738 kind of thing never happened, and now here I am in the middle of one!
 739 There ought to be a book written about me, that there ought! And when I
 740 grow up, I'll write one--but I'm grown up now,' she added in a sorrowful
 741 tone; 'at least there's no room to grow up any more HERE.'
 742 
 743 'But then,' thought Alice, 'shall I NEVER get any older than I am
 744 now? That'll be a comfort, one way--never to be an old woman--but

 746 
 747 'Oh, you foolish Alice!' she answered herself. 'How can you learn
 748 lessons in here? Why, there's hardly room for YOU, and no room at all

 865 
 866 'The first thing I've got to do,' said Alice to herself, as she wandered
 867 about in the wood, 'is to grow to my right size again; and the second

 869 the best plan.'
 870 
 871 It sounded an excellent plan, no doubt, and very neatly and simply
 872 arranged; the only difficulty was, that she had not the smallest idea
 873 how to set about it; and while she was peering about anxiously among
 874 the trees, a little sharp bark just over her head made her look up in a

 876 
 877 An enormous puppy was looking down at her with large round eyes, and
 878 feebly stretching out one paw, trying to touch her. 'Poor little thing!'
 879 said Alice, in a coaxing tone, and she tried hard to whistle to it; but
 880 she was terribly frightened all the time at the thought that it might be
 881 hungry, in which case it would be very likely to eat her up in spite of
 882 all her coaxing.
 883 
 884 Hardly knowing what she did, she picked up a little bit of stick, and
 885 held it out to the puppy; whereupon the puppy jumped into the air off
 886 all its feet at once, with a yelp of delight, and rushed at the stick,
 887 and made believe to worry it; then Alice dodged behind a great thistle,
 888 to keep herself from being run over; and the moment she appeared on the
 889 other side, the puppy made another rush at the stick, and tumbled head
 890 over heels in its hurry to get hold of it; then Alice, thinking it was
 891 very like having a game of play with a cart-horse, and expecting every
 892 moment to be trampled under its feet, ran round the thistle again; then
 893 the puppy began a series of short charges at the stick, running a very
 894 little way forwards each time and a long way back, and barking hoarsely
 895 all the while, till at last it sat down a good way off, panting, with
 896 its tongue hanging out of its mouth, and its great eyes half shut.
 897 
 898 This seemed to Alice a good opportunity for making her escape; so she
 899 set off at once, and ran till she was quite tired and out of breath, and

 901 
 902 'And yet what a dear little puppy it was!' said Alice, as she leant
 903 against a buttercup to rest herself, and fanned herself with one of the

 909 
 910 The great question certainly was, what? Alice looked all round her at

 914 herself; and when she had looked under it, and on both sides of it, and

 917 
 918 She stretched herself up on tiptoe, and peeped over the edge of the

 926 CHAPTER V. Advice from a Caterpillar
 927 
 928 The Caterpillar and Alice looked at each other for some time in silence:
 929 at last the Caterpillar took the hookah out of its mouth, and addressed
 930 her in a languid, sleepy voice.
 931

 933 
 934 This was not an encouraging opening for a conversation. Alice replied,
 935 rather shyly, 'I--I hardly know, sir, just at present--at least I know
 936 who I WAS when I got up this morning, but I think I must have been
 937 changed several times since then.'
 938 
 939 'What do you mean by that?' said the Caterpillar sternly. 'Explain
 940 yourself!'
 941 
 942 'I can't explain MYSELF, I'm afraid, sir' said Alice, 'because I'm not
 943 myself, you see.'
 944 
 945 'I don't see,' said the Caterpillar.
 946 
 947 'I'm afraid I can't put it more clearly,' Alice replied very politely,
 948 'for I can't understand it myself to begin with; and being so many
 949 different sizes in a day is very confusing.'
 950 
 951 'It isn't,' said the Caterpillar.
 952 
 953 'Well, perhaps you haven't found it so yet,' said Alice; 'but when you
 954 have to turn into a chrysalis--you will some day, you know--and then
 955 after that into a butterfly, I should think you'll feel it a little
 956 queer, won't you?'
 957 
 958 'Not a bit,' said the Caterpillar.
 959 
 960 'Well, perhaps your feelings may be different,' said Alice; 'all I know
 961 is, it would feel very queer to ME.'
 962 
 963 'You!' said the Caterpillar contemptuously. 'Who are YOU?'
 964 
 965 Which brought them back again to the beginning of the conversation.

 967 short remarks, and she drew herself up and said, very gravely, 'I think,

 969 
 970 'Why?' said the Caterpillar.
 971 
 972 Here was another puzzling question; and as Alice could not think of any

 974 state of mind, she turned away.
 975

 977 to say!'

 982
